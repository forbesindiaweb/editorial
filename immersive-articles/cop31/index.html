Chapter 01 · What a COP isAct I · The rules of the room

The world's biggest climate negotiation

Once a year, the countries that signed the UN climate convention meet to decide what they can do together about global warming. Their meeting is called a COP: a Conference of the Parties.

The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change was agreed in 1992. Its objective is to prevent dangerous human interference with the climate system. The first COP took place in Berlin in 1995.

Today, 198 Parties belong to the Convention, including the European Union. Each has a voice. Decisions are normally adopted by , making progress possible but also painfully slow. A strongly opposed proposal can force negotiators back to the table.

Not a parliament

A COP is a negotiation forum. There is no majority vote, and decisions are normally adopted by consensus.

      Chapter 03 · Three decades of COPAct II · The road to Antalya

      Thirty years of promises, progress and deadlock

      Three decades of climate talks have produced landmark agreements, missed opportunities and repeated promises of stronger action. Each summit has added something to the global response. But each has also left unfinished business for the next.

      The archive

      A chronology of promises and unfinished business

      The climate talks have produced landmark agreements. They have also repeatedly struggled to turn those agreements into cuts in emissions.

      At Kyoto, governments adopted binding targets for many developed countries. Copenhagen exposed the difficulty of agreeing a new global deal. Paris changed the architecture: every country would set and update its own climate plan, while all would work towards common temperature goals.

      More recent summits have confronted fossil fuels, climate finance, adaptation and the losses climate change is already causing. The story is neither one of complete failure nor of promises fulfilled.

      Before the COPs · The Earth Summit

      Rio de Janeiro

      Brazil Treaty

      1992, Rio de Janeiro. The UN Framework Convention on Climate Change is adopted, establishing the international framework for climate negotiations.

      What changed

      Governments agreed a shared objective: to stabilise greenhouse gas concentrations at a level that would prevent dangerous human interference with the climate system. The Convention made the Conference of the Parties its supreme body.

      What remained unresolved

      The Convention set no binding emissions targets for individual countries. How much each country should do, and by when, was left to future negotiations.

      COP1 · The first summit

      Berlin

      Germany COP decision

      1995, Berlin, COP1. The first COP launches the ongoing negotiation process.

      What changed

      The Parties met for the first time as the Convention's governing body. Their Berlin Mandate opened talks on stronger commitments for developed countries.

      What remained unresolved

      What those commitments would be. The Mandate introduced no new commitments for developing countries, and the question of who should act, and by how much, stayed open.

      Japan Treaty · Protocol

      1997, Kyoto, COP3. The Kyoto Protocol establishes binding emissions-reduction targets for many developed countries.

      What changed

      For the first time, countries accepted legally binding, quantified limits on their emissions under a climate treaty.

      What remained unresolved

      Its limited coverage and the challenge of wider participation. Developing countries took on no binding targets, and bringing every major emitter into one system remained an open question.

      $100bnA year by 2020 for developing countries: the promise developed countries made in Copenhagen
      2022The first year the goal was met, two years after the target date

      What changed

      The Accord recorded political support for keeping warming below 2°C, and a promise by developed countries to mobilise $100 billion a year for developing countries by 2020. Countries were invited to list their own emissions targets and actions.

      What remained unresolved

      There was no comprehensive, legally binding successor to Kyoto. The divisions between developed and developing countries over who should cut, and who should pay, were left exposed. The finance promise was a target, not money delivered.

      2015
      COP21

      Paris

      France Treaty · Agreement

      2015, Paris, COP21. The Paris Agreement establishes the common temperature goals of well below 2°C and pursuing 1.5°C, with countries submitting and updating their own climate plans.

      What changed

      Every Party, not only developed countries, now prepares its own and updates it every five years. A assesses collective progress every five years.

      What remained unresolved

      The obligations to prepare, communicate and report on a plan are binding. Achieving the targets inside each plan is not enforceable in the same way. Whether the plans add up to the shared goals is left to each stocktake to measure.

      COP26

      Glasgow

      United Kingdom COP decisions

      2021, Glasgow, COP26. The Glasgow Climate Pact includes language on phasing down unabated coal power, but falls short of an agreement to phase out fossil fuels.

      What changed

      A negotiated COP text named coal power directly. The Pact called for accelerating efforts towards the phase-down of unabated coal power and the phase-out of inefficient fossil-fuel subsidies.

      What remained unresolved

      "Phase-down", not "phase-out", and only for unabated coal power. There was no agreement to phase out fossil fuels as a whole.

      COP27

      Sharm El-Sheikh

      Egypt COP decisions

      2022, Sharm El-Sheikh, COP27. Countries agree to establish a dedicated fund for loss and damage, recognising the climate harms already confronting vulnerable nations.

      Loss and damageHarm caused by climate impacts that cannot be avoided through mitigation or adaptation, and international efforts to address it. The term is wider than any single fund.

      What changed

      Parties decided to establish new funding arrangements for loss and damage, including a dedicated fund. Responding to harm already happening became a recognised part of the response, alongside cutting emissions and .

      What remained unresolved

      The decision created the fund but not its rules. Who would contribute, who could draw on it and how it would work were left to a transitional committee. The fund was made operational at COP28 a year later.

      2023, Dubai, COP28. The first global stocktake calls for transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems, a major diplomatic development, but without a binding global timetable.

      After 28 summits, a negotiated decision addressed fossil fuels as a whole in energy systems, not only coal. On the conference's opening day, Parties also made the loss and damage fund operational.

      Parties are called on to contribute "in a nationally determined manner". The text sets a direction, not a binding global timetable, and does not say how fast each country should move.

        Three treaties from this archive. One roof in Antalya.

        One summit. Five bodies at work.

        COP31 is the name on the building. Inside, several related but legally distinct bodies meet under the same roof.

        Who decides what

        Three treaties, three governing bodies, two shared engines

        COP31 governs the 1992 climate convention. CMA8 is the eighth meeting of the Parties to the Paris Agreement. CMP21 serves the Kyoto Protocol. Two permanent subsidiary bodies, SBSTA and SBI, each hold their 65th session to support scientific, technical and implementation work.

        Chapter 05 · The state of thingsAct II · The road to Antalya

        The planet is nearing a climate line

        The Paris Agreement commits countries to hold long-term global warming well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5°C.

        One year is not the line

        1.5°C is a long-term goal, not a single-year threshold.

        The latest verified annual figures are unsettling. Global temperature in 2024 reached about 1.55°C above the 1850–1900 average. In 2025 it was about 1.43°C; the three-year average for 2023–25 was 1.48°C.

        These are short-period figures. A single hot year above 1.5°C does not, by itself, establish that the Paris long-term threshold has been crossed. But the margin is narrowing.

        The consequences

        What a warmer world means for people

        The scientific assessments describe patterns across regions and decades. They are the evidence base for the adaptation, finance and loss and damage questions on the table in Antalya.

        1. Heat

          Hot extremes, including heatwaves, have become more frequent and more intense across most land regions since the 1950s. The IPCC finds human-caused climate change is the main driver.

        2. Heavy rain and flooding

          Heavy rainfall has become more frequent and intense over most land areas with enough observations. Whether it becomes a damaging flood also depends on land use, drainage and defences.

        3. Drought

          Human-caused climate change has contributed to increases in agricultural and ecological droughts in some regions.

        4. Food

          Climate change has reduced food and water security, hindering efforts to meet development goals.

        5. Vulnerability

          About 3.3 to 3.6 billion people live in contexts that are highly vulnerable to climate change. Communities that have contributed least to it are often among the most affected.

        A note on single events

        Attribution studies can estimate how far climate change made a specific heatwave, flood or drought more likely or more intense. Without such a study, this immersive does not attribute an individual disaster to climate change.

        The questions COP31 cannot avoid

        Six issues will decide whether Antalya moves the world closer to its goals. Some are measured in degrees, some in dollars, some in gigawatts.

        Jump to an issue

        AThe emissions gapBClimate financeCAdaptationDLoss and damageEFossil fuelsFThe transition

        AThe emissions gap

        On today's course, the world heads well past 2°C.

        UNEP's Emissions Gap Report 2026, "The Point of Return", estimates how much the world would warm this century under different assumptions. These are projections of future warming, not observed temperatures.

        The UNFCCC's 2026 synthesis covers 127 new national climate plans, representing 154 Parties and about 71% of 2019 global emissions.

        The lowest-overshoot pathway shows what would be required, not what is expected. Even on that pathway, warming would exceed 1.5°C before any return, and a return is not guaranteed. The gap is the story.

        BClimate finance

        A promise is not a payment.

        A 2009 promise to mobilise $100 billion a year for developing countries was first met in 2022. Governments have since agreed larger goals for 2035. These figures describe different parts of the climate-finance framework, and they cannot be read as one series.

        A broader call to scale up finance from all public and private sources to at least $1.3 trillion a year by 2035. A wider scope than the $300 billion goal, and not money delivered.

        CAdaptation

        Preparing for impacts costs far more than is flowing.

        Flood barriers, heat plans, drought-resistant farming, early-warning systems and stronger healthcare require money and implementation now. COP31 must advance work on measuring adaptation and backing the commitment to scale adaptation finance.

        DLoss and damage

        The first requests to the fund were more than eight times what it could offer.

        The Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage helps developing countries that are particularly vulnerable to the adverse effects of climate change respond to economic and non-economic loss and damage. It was agreed at Sharm El-Sheikh and made operational at Dubai.

        EFossil fuels and the transition

        Agreed in principle. Disputed in practice.

        COP28 called on countries to transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems. How to turn that call into action remains contested. In 2025, fossil fuels still supplied roughly 86% of total global energy supply, on the Energy Institute's measure.

        Not every statement about fossil fuels carries the same weight. Four kinds of action are often confused.

        1. Adopted COP decision
          Adopted by consensus: a collective direction, not national quotas

          The COP28 global stocktake call to transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems. The adopted COP30 text in Belém contains no fossil-fuel transition roadmap.

        2. Under negotiation
          Not agreed

          Any COP31 text on how to take the transition forward is under negotiation. As of 11 November 2026, nothing on it has been adopted in Antalya.

        3. Voluntary initiatives
          Outside the formal decisions

          Brazil's COP30 presidency launched voluntary roadmaps on the fossil-fuel transition and halting deforestation on 3 November 2026. The Santa Marta conference of April 2026 is another voluntary forum (see F).

        4. National energy policy
          Decided by each government

          Countries' own choices, from new solar capacity to new oil development, such as the Gabar announcement in Türkiye covered in Chapter 06.
