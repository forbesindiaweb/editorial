How the hack happened

One laptop. $6.3bn gone.

Photo: Shutterstock

Binance co-chief executive Richard Teng says the initial investigation suggests the attack may have started with a compromised DevOps engineer’s laptop.

Raghu Nandakumara of cybersecurity firm Illumio says the engineer may have been duped into taking a fake test, with malware landing on the laptop while they did it.

Because the laptop already had high-level security privileges, it became the attacker’s route into the production network.

From there, the attacker got into a service container, a critical internal system that manages exchange operations.

Inside the container, the attacker submitted unauthorised withdrawal transactions, draining Binance’s wallets.

Teng says the attack involved more than one person, who used sophisticated methods to hide their tracks, including a self-destruct mechanism.

The suspect

All eyes on Pyongyang

It is not yet confirmed who is behind the attack. But blockchain analysis firms TRM Labs and Chainalysis have pointed to North Korea.

North Korea has always denied involvement in such attacks.

TRM Labs says the attack resembles earlier heists linked to a North Korean hacking group known as Lazarus, or TraderTraitor, including the $1.5bn Bybit hack in 2025.

TRM Labs’ Ari Redbord says North Korea has stolen about $6bn from crypto over the past five years. If the attribution holds, it took more than that in a single attack.

Oxford University’s Dr Edward Howell says North Korea has a state-run cyber warfare agency, and that about 40–50% of its weapons of mass destruction programmes are funded by cyber and cryptocurrency theft.
