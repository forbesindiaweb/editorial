Hello reader,

It has been an unsettling week for artificial intelligence—or “super intelligence”, as Donald Trump wants it called in US government documents. But a new name does little to resolve the increasingly urgent question: Can AI be contained?

The enthusiasm for agentic AI—systems that can act on our behalf—is now accompanied by uncomfortable questions about what happens when those systems overstep their instructions. Following the breach involving OpenAI agents at Hugging Face, and several others, Australia disclosed this week that an OpenAI agent had gained unauthorised access to a government health-statistics portal in June. It could be the first known instance of an AI agent hacking a government website.

Those concerns also reached the United Nations Security Council on September 23, where Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, OpenAI’s Sam Altman and others called for international cooperation to manage increasingly powerful AI. Setting limits while encouraging innovation will be a difficult balancing act—and one that no country or company can manage alone.

The question now is whether the US and China can look beyond their rivalry long enough to agree on safeguards—and turn shared concerns into meaningful commitments.

Here’s the week in AI—and why its growing capabilities deserve closer scrutiny:

Australia reveals AI breach of government portal

An OpenAI agent gained unauthorised access to non-public files on Australia’s Medicare medical-statistics portal while researching public healthcare spending, the government disclosed this week. The breach occurred in June, but authorities were notified only in September.

Prime Minister Anthony Albanese criticised the delay and said investigators would examine why government systems failed to detect the intrusion. OpenAI said it found no evidence of patient records being accessed—the portal held aggregated healthcare data. Authorities are checking whether other government websites were affected, raising questions about how developers contain autonomous agents and report incidents when those controls fail.

Norway leads call for stronger AI oversight

Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre and Finnish President Alexander Stubb spearheaded a declaration backed by 22 countries seeking stronger international oversight of advanced AI. Presented alongside the UN General Assembly, it calls for independent evaluations, transparent safety measures and shared reporting of serious incidents. It also proposes exploring an international oversight institution.

Concerns include autonomous cyberattacks, assistance in developing biological weapons and lethal autonomous weapons. Støre wants the initiative taken up by smaller international forums, including the G7. But neither the US nor China signed, leaving a significant gap between the declaration’s ambitions and the participation needed to realise them.

AI leaders take their warnings to the UN

The debate over AI safety reached the UN Security Council this week, with Anthropic chief Dario Amodei warning that poorly managed systems could threaten humanity. OpenAI’s Sam Altman called for international cooperation, while researcher Yoshua Bengio stressed the urgency of the risks.

Their interventions highlighted concerns that increasingly capable systems could become harder to supervise. Washington, however, opposed a global regulatory framework, favouring cooperation on domestic capabilities and best practices. The meeting exposed the distance between recognising a shared threat and agreeing on how to address it. Warnings about future loss of control remain distinct from evidence that it has occurred.

Can Trump and Xi find common ground on AI?

Donald Trump’s September 24 state dinner for Xi Jinping brought technology’s power brokers into the diplomatic spotlight, with Sam Altman, Jensen Huang and Elon Musk among the invited guests. Both presidents identified AI as an area for cooperation, following US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent’s proposal for reporting AI incidents with national-security implications.

At the UN Security Council, Altman outlined how shared standards could accommodate national autonomy: Governments should decide how to incorporate them into domestic law, while agreeing internationally on evidence, safeguards and oversight. No comprehensive safety agreement had emerged, however. Can two countries competing for technological supremacy accept common rules that might constrain their ambitions?

AI raises risks for consumers and the financial system

Banks including NatWest and Bank of America warned this week that shopping agents could expose consumers to scams, fraud and privacy breaches, with protections lagging behind the technology. Separately, Columbia Business School professor Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh flagged potential systemic risks from financing the AI infrastructure boom, estimated to require more than $10 trillion through 2032. Greater borrowing, complex funding arrangements and uncertain revenues could spread losses beyond technology companies if demand disappoints. Neither warning signals an imminent crisis, but both highlight the need for safeguards as AI becomes more deeply embedded in finance.
